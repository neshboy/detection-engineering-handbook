# V1 → V2 Changelog

**Status:** Architecture-level seed entry. This file records the *structural* decisions made when synthesizing V2's `BOOK-INDEX.md`, `STYLE-GUIDE.md`, and `TERMINOLOGY.md` from three independent architecture proposals and two independent style/glossary proposals. It will be extended with content-level changes once V2 chapters are actually written — nothing below describes prose changes, only structure and process.

## Why V2 exists

V1 (`release-v1/`) was produced by 55 independent single-pass agents with no shared style contract, no cross-unit review, and no mechanism forcing diagrams or screenshots to become real files. The topic list itself was broadly reasonable — the problems were structural: no reviewer ever caught drift, no diagram among 89 Mermaid sources was ever rendered, no screenshot among 71 placeholders was ever captured, the same concept (PowerShell logging, DNS tunnelling, retention, SIEM migration) was sometimes explained differently in two different parts with no cross-reference, and some parts ended up thinner than others purely from spreading effort across too many same-weight units. V2 fixes each of these at the structural level — in the production model, the file-naming and ID scheme, and the part ordering — rather than trusting the next single-pass author to get it right.

## Structural changes, and why each is better for the reader

### 1. Part count grew from V1's 50 flat, ungrouped parts to 48 parts organized into 10 labeled sections

V1 had no grouping at all, which made dependency order and reviewer/section-owner assignment invisible. V2 groups Parts 1–48 into ten labeled sections (Foundations; Data & Pipeline Foundations; Windows/Identity/Endpoint; Network & Application; AI Security; Detection-as-Code & Query Languages; Advanced Analytic Techniques; Threat Hunting; Testing & Quality; Applied Compromise Models). A reader — or a section owner assigning reviewers — can now see at a glance which parts are mutually dependent and in what order they need to be authored and read.

### 2. Every domain part now states its scope boundary against its neighbors, split along a telemetry-layer vs. analytic-layer line

V1's Windows/Sysmon/PowerShell/Endpoint parts (and Network/DNS, and the original single Cloud/AI parts) listed overlapping topics with no stated division of labor — exactly the seam where a single-pass, unreviewed process silently duplicates or contradicts itself. V2 makes the division explicit and mechanical: Parts 8–10 (Windows, Sysmon, PowerShell) are telemetry-layer only ("what the data is and where it lies to you"); Part 11 (Endpoint) is the analytic layer built on top of them, explicitly cross-OS; Part 14 (Network) and Part 15 (DNS) each exclusively own DNS-tunnelling/rarity logic rather than both listing it. A reader trying to answer "what does 4688 give me before I install anything" now has exactly one part that answers that, stated up front.

### 3. Parsers, Normalisation, and Time moved from the end of the book to immediately after the telemetry survey (new Parts 5–7)

In V1's ordering (and in the sponsor draft TOC all three architecture proposals were given), Correlation Engineering, Testing, and False Negative Engineering all assume clock skew, schema drift, and parser failure as background knowledge dozens of parts before those concepts are formally taught. This is a genuine dependency-order defect, not a style preference — a reader moving front-to-back hit unexplained forward references repeatedly. V2 teaches these three foundational concepts once, right after the telemetry survey that already gestures at them, and every later part references them instead of re-explaining them in miniature.

### 4. Threat Intelligence, Baselining, Correlation, and Risk-Based Detection reordered into an explicit dependency chain (Parts 30–33)

Risk-Based Detection's own scope treats "threat intel confidence" and "statistical rarity" as scoring inputs, but V1 and the sponsor draft taught Threat Intelligence roughly fifteen parts *after* Risk-Based Detection. V2 orders these four parts as mechanical correlation → statistical baselining ("rarity") → external context (threat intel) → the synthesis technique that weights all three plus identity/asset context. Nothing in this reordering required new content — it only required noticing, and fixing, a forward-reference bug.

### 5. Identity split into two parts (Access & Authentication vs. Directory/Privilege/Kerberos); Cloud split into two parts (Identity & SaaS vs. Infrastructure); AI split into two parts (Telemetry/Audit-Trail vs. Detection Engineering)

Each of these three splits addresses the same failure mode: a single part trying to cover fundamentally different telemetry sources, protocols, and audiences (AD/Kerberos analysts vs. cloud IAM engineers; IdP sign-in-log triage vs. Kerberos ticket internals; "what should I even log for an LLM system" vs. "how do I detect prompt injection") cannot go deep on either half without becoming a shallow checklist — the specific "some parts thinner than others" risk named as a V1 weakness. Splitting matches how real teams are actually staffed and lets each half carry its own depth budget and its own audience.

### 6. Telemetry Engineering split into two parts by domain family (Part 3: Host & Identity; Part 4: Network/Application/AI)

Twenty heterogeneous telemetry classes scored on eight consistent dimensions in one part was V1's version of a breadth-only, thin-depth survey. Splitting gives Linux/auditd/SSH, proxy, firewall, NDR/NetFlow, and database — sources that get no dedicated deep-dive part anywhere else in the book — genuine page weight instead of a single table row each.

### 7. A short bridging part, Query Language Strategy (Part 23), added before the six query-language parts, carrying canonical detections through all of them

V1's language parts (Sigma, KQL, SPL, AQL, YARA-L, Elastic) were each written independently with no shared example, so a reader migrating platforms had no way to confirm an "equivalent" rule in two languages actually caught the same thing. V2 threads one canonical detection through Part 23 and then through Parts 24–29, so translation fidelity and translation loss are teachable and checkable instead of assumed.

### 8. Detection Autopsy (Part 40) is now an explicit capstone synthesis of "naive rule" examples seeded in their natural source parts, not new material invented from scratch

Several weak, commonly-shipped rules (PowerShell execution = malicious, five failed logins = brute force, foreign country = compromise, long DNS label = tunnel, curl in UA = attacker, unsigned process = malware, rare domain = malicious) now appear first as a teaser Detection Autopsy in their natural home part (PowerShell, Identity, Network, DNS, Web, Endpoint) and are formally dissected once, fully, in the Part 40 capstone. This gives the recurring feature narrative continuity across the book instead of reading as one isolated, disconnected takedown chapter.

### 9. Coverage (Part 41) placed before Quality (Part 42) and Debt (Part 43)

Quality's own metric set treats coverage as a measured input, and Debt is most coherently framed as accumulated Quality/Coverage erosion. V1's ordering (Quality → Debt → Coverage) had Debt and Quality reference a concept (Coverage) the book hadn't taught yet.

### 10. Mandatory per-file production front matter and permanent, position-independent global IDs

Every chapter, detection, and hunt file now carries required front matter (`author`, `reviewer` — a different person/agent, mandatory — `status`, `depends_on`, `last_validated`), and every detection, hunt, and figure gets a permanent global ID (`DET-####`, `HUNT-####`, `FIG-####`) independent of its position in the book, tracked in `DETECTION-INVENTORY.md`, `HUNT-INVENTORY.md`, and `VISUAL-INVENTORY.md`. This is the direct, enforceable fix for V1's known weakness #1 (55 authors, zero reviewers) and for the fact that V1 had no way to survive its *own* renumbering without breaking cross-references — which this synthesis exercise itself demonstrated is a real, recurring risk.

### 11. Diagrams and screenshots are now a build-checkable property of the filesystem, not a hope

Every Mermaid source must be paired with a rendered SVG/PNG at a matching filename, and every screenshot filename must carry a `REAL-LAB-` or `SYNTH-UI-` prefix declaring whether it's a genuine capture or a clearly labeled synthetic mockup, logged in `VISUAL-INVENTORY.md`. A build check fails if a cited figure ID has no rendered file present. This directly converts V1's "89 diagrams, 0 rendered / 71 screenshots, 0 captured" from a promise into a gate.

### 12. One shared style contract and one shared glossary, both binding across all 48 parts

V1 had neither: the same callout ("Engineering Reality," "Detection Autopsy") rendered as bold run-in text, an H2, an H3, and a blockquote depending on the part, and the same term ("benign positive" vs. "benign true positive," bare "coverage" vs. qualified coverage) was used inconsistently across chapters. V2's `STYLE-GUIDE.md` locks all eight recurring callouts to one exact blockquote template each, and `TERMINOLOGY.md` is now the single source of truth every chapter must link back to rather than redefine.

## What this file does not yet cover (as of the original seed entry)

Content-level changes — new detections, corrected technical claims, rewritten prose, added worked examples — were out of scope for the original seed entry above and are now appended below, since all 55 planned parts/appendices have completed the full author → technical-reviewer → adversarial-reviewer → editor pipeline.

## Content-level changes

This section is the evidence that V2 is materially better than a reorganized V1, not just better organized. Every one of the 55 units passed through an adversarial-review pass whose explicit job was to attack the unit's own detections and hunts for bypasses, false positives, and false negatives — and in nearly every unit, that pass found and fixed at least one real, previously-undocumented defect directly in the shipped query logic, not just in prose framing. The summaries below are grouped by the book's ten labeled sections and name the single most significant class of finding per section; each unit's own `review_notes` field carries the full list.

### Foundations (Parts 1–2)

Part 1's DET-01-01 (repeated failed SSH auth) survived adversarial pacing/grouping-key attacks only by having two real, previously-undocumented coverage gaps named as a permanent Blind Spot rather than "fixed" with a threshold tweak: sub-threshold pacing (2 attempts/5 min) and spray-and-distribute across many accounts or sources, neither of which a count threshold can close. Part 2's encoded-PowerShell detection had two real bypasses fixed directly in the query — a verified 107-character alias-based download cradle beat the length threshold, and a renamed or .NET-reflection-executed binary beat the FileName filter — plus a missing False Positive Trap for `-EncodedCommand` use by SCCM/Intune/DSC/backup tooling.

### Data & Pipeline Foundations (Parts 3–7)

Part 5 is the standout: adversarial review caught a genuine logic bug that had survived the first author+reviewer pass — DET-05-01's selection and filter clauses both keyed on the same post-parse field (`user.name`), so the allowlist filter silently never excluded anyone, and the one piece of real evidence available (invoking user = target user = root) had masked the bug from the technical reviewer. It was fixed by correctly splitting invoking-user vs. target-user fields per real ECS convention. Part 3's SSH-burst rule had a sub-threshold evasion blind spot backed by real honeypot evidence (a genuine distributed credential-spray capture) and an OpenSSH-only regex that breaks against any non-OpenSSH daemon; its Detection Test was also non-reproducible (silently skipped password auth with no TTY, fixed by adding `sshpass`). Part 4 alone had six independent real bypasses fixed across its six detections (fixed-window bucketing evasion, QUIC false positives, C2 jitter defeating a beaconing stdev check, percent-encoding bypassing an exploit-path string match, pagination evading a row-count z-score, and UA/IP rotation defeating an MCP-scanner clustering pivot). Part 6 found a genuine KQL correctness bug: `has_any`'s whole-token matching would silently zero-match against punctuated path values like `/hachk.php`, meaning the query as originally drafted would never have fired on its own cited evidence. Part 7's Detection Test was self-defeating (it generated only one event against a `count>5` threshold and could never pass).

### Windows, Identity & Endpoint (Parts 8–13)

Part 9 found that DET-09-01's tool-exclusion list matches `SourceImage` by filename suffix only, so a renamed credential-dumping tool bypasses it exactly like the real attack it's meant to catch — and separately, that its own companion tamper-detection hunt (HUNT-09-01) is defeated by a single live `sysmon64.exe -c` config push adding one exclude rule, which produces zero volume change and leaves the only trace on the same channel the attacker just edited. Part 10 found the fetch-keyword lists in both PowerShell download-cradle detections missed common aliases (`iwr`, `irm`, `wget`, `curl`), letting alias-based one-liners evade both rules outright. Part 11 found LOLBAS/GTFOBins name-based signals are defeated by simple binary renaming, and that a shell-hiding `ExecStart=/bin/bash -c` form bypasses the systemd-unit persistence fix the chapter itself proposed. Part 12 found two unbounded self-joins in its account-takeover composite that would have scanned a SIEM's entire table retention in production, plus a permanent (not episodic) false-positive source from large NAT'd offices. Part 13 found DET-13-04's DCSync detection checked only `SubjectUserSid` and missed the stolen-machine-account-credential replay path entirely — fixed by also validating source IP against each inventoried domain controller's expected address.

### Network & Application (Parts 14–19)

Part 14 is the deepest single adversarial pass in the book: six real, independently-fixed defects across four detections and one hunt, including an out-of-order-indexing corruption risk in a `streamstats` beaconing query, proxy/NAT fan-out false positives, distributed and low-and-slow scan evasion, an architecturally-unlistable Tor-bridge blind spot, and a bidirectional-channel exfiltration gap the ratio-and-volume logic missed entirely. Part 16's editorial pass caught a factual error against its own cited evidence — the Detection Autopsy claimed 6 of 20 honeynet sessions escalated to `possible_success` when the underlying capture actually shows 13. Part 17 found DET-17-01's exact-string VIP-name join is defeated by whitespace, titles, or homoglyphs, and DET-17-02's prior-contact check has a "warm-up" bypass via an innocuous first message. Part 19 found six real CloudTrail bypasses across its six IAM/storage/network/logging detections (array-form wildcard actions, a no-op `Condition` block, `ipProtocol -1` wide-open rules, `PutEventSelectors` silently narrowing an already-enabled trail, and fully-public snapshot shares the original query missed).

### AI Security (Parts 20–21)

Part 20's exfiltration join only matched `tool_call` rows with a populated `external_destination`, completely missing the case where an internally-routed tool call is later forwarded externally by the receiving system itself (an auto-forwarded ticket or outbound webhook) — a structural gap documented as a new Blind Spot rather than silently left uncaught. Part 21 is the other standout of the book: eight distinct, real logic breaks across its eight detections, including a session-boundary guard bug, two dead-signal bugs from a premature length gate and a non-matching regex in the hidden-instruction detector, a trivially-defeated Sybil threshold in the RAG-poisoning rule, a detection signal the query claimed to implement but didn't, a cross-run state gap in the lethal-trifecta scoring, and a baseline-join blind spot that silently dropped newly issued API keys from the token-spike detector.

### Detection-as-Code & Query Languages (Parts 22–29)

Part 22 found a total, unconditional bypass of its sudo-baseline detection: once an interpreter like `bash` is baselined for a (target_user, host) pair, `sudo -u <user> bash -c '<anything>'` matches the same allowed binary path forever, regardless of what the command actually does. This same underlying weakness — filtering an allowlist by bare process name with no path or signing check — turned out to be the single most-repeated real finding in the entire book: Part 23 found Sigma's `contains` modifier does literal substring matching, not a bitwise AND, so a combined-rights access mask silently evades a listed hex value; Part 28 found the YARA-L filename-only allowlist is defeated by renaming a tool to `svchost.exe`; Part 29 found the same gap in its native GrantedAccess allowlist; and Appendix A5 found it independently a fourth time while restating all six backends side by side. Parts 25 and 27 each separately caught that a `GrantedAccess` field's hex-vs-decimal *format* (not just its presence) has to be verified per data connector, since a DSM emitting decimal would silently zero out the query forever without ever throwing an error.

### Advanced Analytic Techniques (Parts 30–33)

Part 30 found a real join-key misalignment (`SubjectLogonId` vs. `TargetLogonId`) in its five-stage correlation chain plus an SPL syntax error (`table` doesn't support `AS` aliasing) — both would have caused the correlation to silently fail to fire in production. Part 31 found a divide-by-zero guard in its SSH baseline that silently required 4+ failures/hour on any zero-variance account, defeating the chapter's own headline low-and-slow example. Part 32 found DET-32-01's `TimeGenerated` filter on the threat-intelligence indicator table silently dropped every aged or standing indicator before the join ever ran, meaning the detection could only ever match brand-new indicators — the opposite of its stated purpose. Part 33 found its kill-chain stage-weight mapping was duplicated across the `SELECT` and `WHERE` clauses, risking a silent score/threshold desync on any future edit to just one copy.

### Threat Hunting (Parts 34–39)

Part 34 found that a single environmental change (`Defaults requiretty` in sudoers) collapses its zero-baseline TTY signal to pure noise, and that a no-pty `ssh account@host "sudo <cmd>"` invocation evades the detection completely. Part 35 found its Sigma rule missed the single most common OpenSSH pre-authentication scanner log line, meaning it would under-fire on the most ordinary case rather than the exotic one. Part 36 found a duplicate-exclusion design choice produces a false negative against persistent attackers who replay an identical exploit payload. Part 37 found an evasion where an attacker paced at 8–9 attempts per 5 minutes could run the same dictionary attack indefinitely without ever crossing the burst threshold — and noted explicitly that lowering the threshold to catch it would reintroduce an already-documented typo false positive. Part 38 found its TTY-presence split is defeated by deliberate TTY-stripping (`setsid`, `at`, `cron`, `ssh -T`). Part 39 found its fixed, clock-aligned correlation windows are trivially evaded by splitting an attack across a window boundary.

### Testing & Quality (Parts 40–43)

Part 40 (Detection Autopsy) found a PowerShell alias bypass, an impossible-travel blind spot for same-country attacker infrastructure, and a false-positive risk in its own new curl/User-Agent composite detection that required splitting the rule into exploit-only and admin-generic path variants. Part 41 found a schema/display-string mismatch in its coverage-staleness query that would silently return zero rows forever, plus a trivial bypass where `last_validated` can be hand-edited with no real re-test behind it. Part 42 found a genuine null-propagation bug: DET-42-02's original 30-minute pre-filter caused `summarize` over zero rows to return a null `LastSeen`, which made the ">20 minutes overdue" check silently evaluate false for any outage longer than the lookback window — the exact case the canary detection exists to catch. Part 43 found its clock-aligned 5-minute bucketing structurally misses a burst split across a window boundary, and that its per-source/account scoping gives distributed password spraying a total bypass regardless of volume.

### Applied Compromise Models (Parts 44–48)

Part 44 found that its `tactic_count >= 2` multi-tactic gate never actually discriminates against the book's own real honeynet dataset — every qualifying session already carries three tactics, not two — and separately misses a single-technique, previously-scouted attacker who sends only the exploit-shaped request. Part 45 found its shadow-copy-deletion detection missed `wbadmin delete backup -keepVersions:0` entirely, plus a WMI-based (`Win32_ShadowCopy`) deletion path with no process-command-line footprint at all. Part 46 found that DET-46-02 computed a `distinct_commands` field but never actually used it in the query logic, making its "novel command" claim false until fixed. Part 47 found a real Kusto `has`-vs-`contains` substring-match bug that silently missed nearly all HTTPS-based downloads in its lineage-plus-download detection. Part 48 found a strict `<` ordering comparison that produced a false negative against the chapter's own worked case study, where the diverging agent decision and the sensitive tool call are logged as the same event.

### Appendices (A1–A7)

Appendix A1 added three new Blind Spots the original draft implied were already closed: BYOVD defeats the Sysmon driver-load signature check by design, APC/thread-hijack injection produces no CreateRemoteThread record at all, and service-configuration persistence via `sc config` bypasses the audited service-creation events entirely. Appendix A4's illustrative exploit-path Sigma rule matched against `url.path` alone even though ECS defines query-string content in a separate `url.query` field — meaning its SQLi-style pattern could never have matched a real query string, verified against the book's own GPON-probe evidence. Appendix A5, restating all six query-language implementations of the canonical LSASS-access detection side by side, is where the book's single most-repeated finding becomes visible at a glance: every one of the six backends filters its exclusion/allowlist by bare process name only, with no path or signing check, independently rediscovered in Parts 22–29 and confirmed once more here. Appendix A6 found a SQL bug in its /24-derivation logic and a factual mismatch where a cited "naturally-occurring" 2-IP evasion example didn't actually match what the real evidence file showed. Appendix A7 found its own visual-asset render-status table had gone stale, still listing two diagrams as unrendered after both had already been rendered and embedded in their source chapters — a small but pointed reminder that even the book's own QA-tracking apparatus needs the same adversarial scrutiny as its detections.
